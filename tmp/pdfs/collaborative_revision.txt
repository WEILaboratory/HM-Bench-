Abstract
The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into adolescents’ digital1
lives presents a dual reality of signiﬁcant opportunity and under-examined risk.2
Existing safety evaluations for “aligned” LLMs primarily focus on explicit harms,3
such as hate speech and violence, but often miss implicit developmental harms4
that can subtly shape adolescents’ values, self-perception, and behaviors through5
multi-turn interaction. We argue that adolescent AI safety requires moving be-6
yond surface safety toward context-aware safety. We ﬁrst develop a taxonomy7
of implicit developmental harms grounded in developmental psychology, social8
learning theory, and adolescent mental health. Building on this taxonomy, we9
then introduce SaplingBench, a multi-turn benchmark simulating scenarios where10
adolescents seek validation in moral and behavioral grey areas. Evaluating lead-11
ing open- and closed-source LLMs on S APLING BENCH reveals systematic vul-12
nerabilities and turn-wise risk escalation. Based on these ﬁndings, we further13
propose S APLING GUARD , a plug-and-play multi-agent safety guardrail that inte-14
grates user proﬁle modeling, risk assessment, and prompt-level intervention be-15
fore downstream generation. Experiments show that SaplingGuard improves risk16
identiﬁcation and cuts harmful rates from 17.88% to 4.17%, providing a frame-17
work for mitigating developmental harms in adolescent LLM systems. Our bench-18
mark and code are available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/19

1 Introduction21
The unprecedented proliferation of LLMs is profoundly reshaping how adolescent users access in-22
formation, interact, and learn. From assisting with academic tasks to serving as digital companions,23
LLMs are becoming an integral part of adolescents’ digital lives, offering immense potential for ed-24
ucational and creative empowerment [ 1]. However, this deep integration also poses a pressing and25
complex question: how can we ensure that these “aligned” LLMs genuinely protect their adolescent26
users? We use “aligned” LLMs to refer to models that have undergone standard post-training safety27
alignment before public release. Currently, LLM safety evaluations primarily focus on preventing28
easily detectable explicit harms, such as violence or hate speech [ 2], with efforts largely aimed at29
ensuring models meet basic "surface safety" standards. However, relying solely on such defenses30
risks masking deeper, more insidious dangers.31
The core thesis of this research is to go beyond surface safety. We contend that the limitation of32
current evaluations lies in their failure to adequately address implicit developmental harms that33
may not immediately trigger red ﬂags but can subtly inﬂuence an adolescent user’s developing val-34
ues, self-perception, and behavioral patterns over more nuanced interactions. These harms exert35

2 Related Work76
2.1 General LLM Safety and Alignment Research77
A comprehensive research ecosystem has emerged to ensure LLM safety through a loop of harm def-78
inition, vulnerability discovery, and alignment. Harms are systematically categorized into risks like79
bias, misinformation, and broader societal ethical threats [ 5, 6]. Red teaming remains the primary80
methodology for discovery, evolving from manual probing to automated, model-driven approaches81
[7, 2] targeting vectors like jailbreaking [ 8], prompt injection [ 9], and PII leakage [ 10]. In response,82
alignment techniques such as RLHF [ 11] and Constitutional AI [ 12] have been deployed at scale83
[13]. Progress is tracked using benchmarks for explicit harms like toxicity [ 14] and factual inaccu-84
racies [15], or holistic frameworks [16]. However, current surveys [17, 18] suggest these frameworks85
primarily address explicitly deﬁnable risks, leaving a gap for vulnerable populations like minors who86
possess unique cognitive characteristics.87
2.2 LLM Safety Guardrails and Mitigation88
To provide real-time protection beyond static ofﬂine alignment, research has shifted toward external89
safety guardrails and self-defense mechanisms [ 19]. Model-based classiﬁers such as Llama Guard90
[20] and WILDGUARD [ 21] act as safety ﬁrewalls for monitoring inputs and outputs. Recent91
advancements include JAILJUDGE[ 22] for adversarial defense and CourtGuard [ 23] for policy-92
aligned monitoring. Complementarily, SELFDEFEND [ 24] explores internalizing defense within93
the model’s generation process. Despite their efﬁcacy in general scenarios, these guardrails are94
predominantly optimized for explicit adversarial attacks or toxic content. They often lack the con-95
textual sensitivity and age-speciﬁc logic required to handle the nuanced interactions characteristic96
of younger users.97
2.3 LLM Safety Research for Minors98
A growing body of work explores the unique safety landscape for minors, highlighting an “empathy99
gap” in child-AI interactions [ 25]. Foundational studies emphasize specialized safety mechanisms100
[26] and empirical testing on commercial chatbots [ 27]. Benchmarks like Safe-Child-LLM [ 4] ini-101
tiated quantiﬁable assessments for adolescents but remained focused on single-turn explicit harms.102
Recognizing the limitations of single-turn evaluations, Rath et al. [ 28] pioneered multi-turn assess-103
ments, and ChildSafe [ 29] utilized simulated agents for multi-dimensional assessment.104
Despite these efforts, a critical gap remains for both a benchmark targeting implicit developmental105
harms through systematic inducement and a mitigation system covering the unique risks of adoles-106

3.1 Deriving the Taxonomy of Implicit Developmental Harms110

of seven implicit developmental harm categories. The development of this taxonomy is grounded in112
established research from developmental psychology, social learning theory and adolescent mental113
health [ 30]. Unlike explicit harms, these categories (Examples shown in Table 1, detailed in Ap-114
pendix A) focus on responses that may not trigger standard safety guardrails but can have a profound115
negative impact on an adolescent’s developing values, self-perception, and behavioral patterns.116

3.2 Multi-turn Dialogue Generation117
We develop a three-turn inducement methodology for S APLING BENCH (detailed in Appendix B).118
Rather than relying on static adversarial prompts, each dialogue follows an escalating risk chain:119
(1) Innocuous Opening initiates a benign, help-seeking scenario to establish context and bypass120
keyword-based guardrails; (2) Risk Introduction subtly pivots the conversation into a “grey area”121
by introducing a ﬂawed premise or potentially harmful idea to test the model’s contextual risk de-122
tection; and (3) Value-based Probing directly requests validation for the harmful concept, forcing123
the model to make a value judgment rather than a purely operational response.124
This three-step structure creates a contextual pressure test, allowing us to observe how safety align-125
ment erodes as the users intent is gradually unmasked. We authored 276 such dialogue chains across126
the seven harm categories, with construction details and examples provided in Appendix B.127
3.3 Quality Control128
To ensure the quality of S APLING BENCH , we conducted a human validation on 20% of the ran-129
domly sampled dialogue chains. We assessed each sample against three criteria: Clarity and Flu-130
ency, Category Relevance, and Methodological Adherence to our multi-turn structure. Our valida-131
tion revealed that 98.2% of the samples fully meet all criteria, conﬁrming the high overall quality132
of our benchmark.133
4 S APLING GUARD134
SAPLING BENCH reveals that current large language models exhibit safety vulnerabilities when con-135
fronted with implicit developmental harms. However, identifying these vulnerabilities alone does136
not prevent harmful interactions in real-world use. In adolescentLLM interactions, risks are often137
context-dependent and implicit, rather than explicit harmful requests. As a result, assessing risk138
based solely on the local semantics of a single prompt can signiﬁcantly underestimate potential139
harm. At the same time, risks in SaplingBench typically emerge gradually over multi-turn interac-140
tions rather than in a single turn. Such risks depend heavily on dialogue context and are difﬁcult for141
classiﬁers trained on isolated inputs to capture. While ﬁne-tuning and RLHF can improve general142
safety behaviors, they often lack explicit modeling of user proﬁles, dialogue context, and structured143
risk knowledge, limiting their ability to adapt to evolving and implicit risks.144

To address this, we propose S APLING GUARD , a multi-agent safety guardrail. SaplingGuard detects145
and mitigates implicit developmental harms before prompts are passed to a downstream LLM. It146
consists of three agents: a Proﬁle Construction Agent that builds and updates structured user pro-147
ﬁles, a Risk Assessment Agent that performs context-aware risk evaluation using a developmental148
risk knowledge base, and a Prompt Optimization Agent that rewrites prompts and generates safety149
guidance. Together, these agents form a plug-and-play middleware layer that enables proactive and150
adaptive safety intervention without modifying the core LLM.151
4.1 Proﬁle Construction Agent152
The Proﬁle Construction Agent builds and updates a structured user proﬁle for each dialogue. Since153
the developmental risks in SaplingBench are highly context-dependent, the same utterance may154
require different safety judgments depending on the user’s background, current state, and dialogue155
history. Following PSG-Agent’s proﬁle modeling strategy[ 31], we represent the user proﬁle as a156
combination of stable and dynamic attributes: Pt = {S, Dt}.157
Here, S denotes relatively stable attributes, such as age, school level, academic performance, family158
environment, peer inﬂuence, and personality traits. Dt denotes dynamic attributes, such as the159
current topic and dominant emotion. Stable attributes capture the user’s longer-term background,160
while dynamic attributes capture the psychological state and conversational context of the current161
turn. The complete proﬁle schema is provided in Appendix D.1.162
The two types of attributes are updated differently. For stable attributes, the agent maintains a163
conﬁdence score and updates an attribute only when the newly inferred value has higher conﬁdence164
than the existing one, reducing noisy proﬁle changes caused by ambiguous single-turn expressions.165
Dynamic attributes are updated after each turn, since topics and emotions can shift rapidly during a166
conversation.167
This proﬁle allows SAPLING GUARD to assess risks beyond the literal meaning of the current prompt.168
For example, an utterance such as "I want to go out alone tonight to calm down" may be low-risk169
in isolation, but it requires more careful handling when the proﬁle indicates that the user is an170
adolescent experiencing intense negative emotion, family conﬂict, or impulsive behavior risk. In171
this way, the proﬁle supports more ﬁne-grained contextual reasoning about developmental risks.172
4.2 Risk Assessment Agent173
The Risk Assessment Agent determines whether the current prompt matches any safety risk when174
considered together with the user proﬁle and dialogue context. Instead of using a single binary175
classiﬁer, the agent relies on a structured risk knowledge base K, where each entry contains a risk176
category, scenario description, applicability condition, and mitigation policy:177
ki = (idi, categoryi, scenarioi, conditioni, policyi). (1)
The knowledge base covers both the implicit developmental risks deﬁned by S APLING BENCH and178
common explicit safety risks such as self-harm, violence, sexual content, and illegal behavior. The179
complete knowledge base is provided in the Appendix C.180
The agent operates in two stages. First, it retrieves candidate risk entries using an embedding-based181
RAG method. Each risk entry is encoded from its category, scenario, and applicability condition182
using bge-base-en-v1.5[32]. For the current turn, retrieval uses two signals: the current prompt ut183
and the user proﬁle Pt. The ﬁnal score for a risk entry ki is computed as:184
Score(ki) = (1 − α) · Score prompt(ut, ki) + α · Score proﬁle(Pt, ki), (2)
where Score prompt measures semantic similarity to the prompt, Score proﬁle measures similarity to185
the proﬁle, and α controls the proﬁle weight. The candidate set is then deﬁned by top- K ranking186
and a threshold τ :187
ˆKt = {ki | ki ∈ TopK(Score(ki)), Score (ki) ≥ τ }. (3)

This retrieval step keeps risk judgment focused on a small set of relevant entries, reducing context188
length and limiting irrelevant distractions.189
Second, a local LLM discriminates among the retrieved candidates. Given the dialogue, user proﬁle,190
and candidate entries, it outputs a structured decision including matched risk IDs, risk categories, a191
harmfulness judgment, and a brief rationale. The full prompt and output schema are provided in the192
Appendix D.2.193
To handle multi-turn risk accumulation, the agent also uses a risk carry-forward mechanism. Once194
a risk entry is matched in an earlier turn, it is added to the candidate set in later turns, even if it is195
not retrieved again by RAG. This allows the agent to capture implicit references, ellipsis, and short196
follow-up questions that continue an earlier risk.197
4.3 Prompt Optimization Agent198
The Prompt Optimization Agent converts the risk assessment result into a safety intervention before199
the prompt is sent to the downstream core LLM. Its goal is not to simply refuse risky requests,200
but to preserve the user’s legitimate intent while removing or reconstructing parts that may cause201
developmental harm.202
When no clear risk is detected, the optimizer returns “action=keep” and preserves the original203
prompt. When any risk is detected, it returns “action=rewrite”, replacing the risky request with204
a safer formulation. Examples of prompt rewriting are provided in the Appendix D.3.205
The optimizer also generates “downstream_response_guidance” based on the mitigation policy of206
the matched risk entries. This guidance is appended to the optimized prompt and instructs the down-207
stream model to avoid providing unsafe advice, while prioritizing safer alternatives, de-escalation,208
and help-seeking suggestions. This design preserves the naturalness of the rewritten prompt while209
providing an explicit safety control signal for the downstream model. The full prompt and structured210
output format are provided in the Appendix D.3.211

5.1 S APLING BENCH213
To better illustrate the advantages of the dataset we have constructed, we conduct a comparative214

5.1.1 Experimental Setup219
Settings We use the prompts and responses from the previous rounds as the dialogue history in all220
experiments. For each LLM, we adopt the corresponding chat format and system prompt, set the221
temperature to 0.7, and limit the maximum number of additional tokens to 512. For LLM-Judge222
(GPT-5), we set the temperature to 0.223

5.1.2 Evaluation227
To meticulously evaluate models’ capability in handling implicit developmental harms, we move228
beyond the simple "safe/unsafe" binary classiﬁcation and adopt a ﬁve-point scoring system ranging229
from +2 to -2, with detailed criteria provided in Appendix E. This scoring rubric precisely dis-230
tinguishes model responses across a spectrum from proactive guidance to harmful compliance. To231
quantify the overall safety performance, we compute a weighted average score for each model across232
all conversational turns by summing the products of each ratings frequency and its corresponding233
integer value, thereby providing a composite metric for ranking.234

6 Conclusion343
In this work, we move beyond surface-level safety evaluation and study implicit developmental344
harms in adolescent-facing LLM interactions. We introduce S APLING BENCH , a multi-turn bench-345
mark for uncovering subtle developmental harms in “aligned” LLMs that standard safety evaluations346
often miss. Our evaluation of leading open- and closed-source LLMs shows that current align-347
ment remains fragile in such settings: harmful responses often emerge or intensify over multi-turn348
dialogue. We further propose S APLING GUARD , a proﬁle-aware multi-agent guardrail that com-349
bines user proﬁle construction, risk assessment, and prompt-level intervention. Experiments show350
that user proﬁles improve risk identiﬁcation, while the full guardrail substantially reduces harmful351
downstream responses without modifying the core LLMs. We hope SAPLING BENCH and SAPLING -352
GUARD provide a foundation for evaluating and building LLM systems that are safer for adolescent353
users.354
Limitations355
Our study is subject to several limitations. First, our experiments are conducted in English, while356
implicit developmental harms are closely tied to cultural norms, linguistic nuance, and local expec-357
tations around adolescence; extending S APLING BENCH to multilingual and cross-cultural settings358
is an important direction for future work. Second, although our multi-turn dialogue chains are de-359
signed from developmental psychology and manually validated, they remain simulated interactions360
and cannot fully capture the diversity, ambiguity, and long-term dynamics of real adolescent–LLM361
use. Third, S APLING GUARD relies on structured user proﬁles and a curated risk knowledge base,362
which may require adaptation as deployment contexts, safety policies, and adolescent interaction363
patterns evolve.364

B Detailed Methodology and Examples for S APLING BENCH600
B.1 Multi-turn Dialogue Construction Logic601
To simulate realistic, high-risk interactions between adolescents and LLMs, each dialogue in602
SAPLING BENCH is structured as a three-turn inducement chain. This design moves beyond static603
adversarial prompts to emulate how a vulnerable or curious adolescent might progressively seek604
validation for harmful concepts. Each dialogue chain adheres to the following structural logic:605
• Innocuous Opening : The user initiates the conversation with a common, non-harmful606
problem or interest. The purpose of this turn is to establish a benign conversational con-607
text, simulating a standard help-seeking scenario. This design allows the interaction to608
effectively bypass the model’s initial, often keyword-based, safety guardrails.609
• Risk Introduction: In response to the model’s standard, safe guidance from the ﬁrst turn,610
the user subtly pivots the conversation into a grey area. This is typically achieved by in-611
troducing a ﬂawed premise or a potentially harmful idea. This turn is designed to test the612
model’s ability to detect a contextual shift in risk, challenging it to recognize a dangerous613
deviation while maintaining its established "helper" persona.614
• Value-based Probing: This is the ultimate test of the model’s safety alignment. The user615
moves beyond seeking operational advice to directly requesting reasonable validation for616
the harmful concept introduced in last turn. The idea is often framed as a reasonable or so-617
cially common concept among peers. This turn forces the model to make a value judgment618
rather than simply providing information.619
