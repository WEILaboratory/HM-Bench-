Progress is tracked using standardized bench-158

However, as highlighted in comprehensive sur-164

2.2 LLM Safety Research for Minors174
Indeed, a growing body of work has begun to175
address this critical entry point, exploring the176
unique safety landscape for minors that general-177
purpose frameworks overlook. Foundational stud-178
ies have established the necessity of specialized179
safety mechanisms through systematic reviews180
(Jiao et al., 2025a) and empirical tests on com-181
mercial chatbots (Saldana et al., 2025). Concur-182
rently, other researchers have conceptually framed183
the core risk of inappropriate responses in child-184
LLM interactions as an "empathy gap" (Kurian,185
2024).186
Building on this, targeted evaluation benchmarks 187
have been proposed to translate these abstract risks 188
into quantifiable assessments. Safe-Child-LLM 189
(Jiao et al., 2025b) is a significant example, craft- 190
ing adversarial prompts for different adolescent 191
age groups, yet its paradigm remains limited to 192
single-turn interactions and explicit harms. Rec- 193
ognizing the limitations of single-turn evaluations, 194
Rath et al. (2025) pioneered the extension of evalu- 195
ation to multi-turn dialogues. However, their work 196
also focused on explicit harms and relied on an 197
automated red-teaming LLM, a method that may 198
be limited in its ability to probe for the subtle risks 199
that require deeper psychological simulation.More 200
recently, ChildSafe (Murali et al., 2025) achieved 201
multi-dimensional, multi-turn assessment via sim- 202
ulated child agents. The common thread in these 203
works is that they primarily test model reactions 204
to explicitly risky scenarios through adversarial or 205
simulatory means. 206
In summary, while there is a growing consensus 207
on the need for specialized, ethics-driven frame- 208
works for children (e.g., Sarukkai, 2025), and eval- 209
uation has already been extended to multi-turn 210
dialogues, a critical methodological gap remains. 211
There is currently no benchmark that specifi- 212
cally targets implicit developmental harms using 213
a systematic methodology of principled induce- 214
ment dialogue chains. Addressing this gap is the 215
core contribution of our work. 216

3.1 Deriving the Taxonomy of Implicit 218
Developmental Harms 219
To systematically probe for potential harms to ado- 220
lescents in LLMs, we first construct a taxonomy 221
of eight implicit developmental harm categories. 222
The development of this taxonomy is grounded in 223
established research from developmental psychol- 224
ogy, social learning theory and adolescent mental 225
health (Lin and Guo, 2024). Unlike explicit harms, 226
these categories (Examples shown in Table 1, de- 227
tailed in Appendix A) focus on responses that may 228
not trigger standard safety guardrails but can have 229
a profound negative impact on a adolescent’s de- 230
veloping values, self-perception, and behavioral 231
patterns. Our eight categories are derived as 232
follows: 233
Normalized Expressions of Risky Behavior 234
refers to model responses that frame dangerous 235
or unhealthy activities as exciting, cool, or as ef- 236

fective shortcuts to problem-solving. According237
to Social Learning Theory (Bandura, 1965; Ban-238
dura and Walters, 1977; Bandura et al., 1986; Ban-239
dura, 2009), adolescents are highly susceptible to240
learning by observing and imitating the actions of241
role models. During a developmental stage char-242
acterized by sensation-seeking and peer validation243
(Steinberg, 2017; Casey et al., 2008; Gardner and244
Steinberg, 2005; Steinberg, 2010), a trusted AI can245
act as a "non-human role model." Its normalization246
of risky behaviors may be misinterpreted by users247
as viable or even desirable actions (Araujo et al.,248
2020).249
Guidance of Moral Disengagement Mecha-250
nisms involves the model validating or reinforc-251
ing a user’s justifications for immoral behavior.252
These justifications, based on Bandura’s theory of253
moral disengagement, include eight mechanisms254
such as moral justification, advantageous compari-255
son, and attribution of blame (Bandura, 2002). As256
adolescents’ cognitive control is still developing257
(Luna et al., 2015) while their emotional systems258
are highly active (Steinberg, 2010), they are more259
easily persuaded by such seemingly rational ex-260
cuses in grey-area situations (Greene et al., 2001).261
This can erode the development of their moral rea-262
soning and potentially lead to increased aggression263
(Paciello et al., 2008).264
Reinforcement of Negative Emotions focuses265
on responses that agree with, confirm, or amplify a266
user’s expressed negative emotions related to fail-267
ure, punishment, comparison, and shame. Adoles-268
cence is a period of heightened emotional volatility269
(Larson et al., 2002), with strong amygdala reac-270
tivity to emotional stimuli (Casey et al., 2008) and271
immature emotion regulation capabilities (Task,272
2008). A model’s reinforcement of pessimistic273
thoughts can exacerbate these negative feedback274
loops and increase the risk of developing psychi-275
atric disorders such as depression and anxiety (Paus276
et al., 2008).277
Expression of Stereotypes pertains to model278
outputs that perpetuate widely circulated but fun-279
damentally incorrect stereotypes about gender, so-280
cial class, or other groups. Adolescence is a sensi-281
tive period for sociocultural learning, during which282
adolescents can absorb these biases uncritically283
(Blakemore and Mills, 2014). Research indicates284
that gender stereotypes can have a significant nega-285
tive impact on students’ academic achievement and286
self-concept (Igbo et al., 2015).287
Shaping of Pathological Body Image and Ma-288
nipulation of Diet includes content that promotes 289
a single, narrow aesthetic standard or recommends 290
unhealthy dieting methods. Such content can eas- 291
ily trigger body dissatisfaction and anxiety in ado- 292
lescents (Vannucci and Ohannessian, 2018). Due 293
to their fragile body consciousness, these sugges- 294
tions may be adopted as concrete dieting behav- 295
iors, potentially leading to eating disorders and 296
co-occurring with other mental health issues like 297
social anxiety and obsessive-compulsive symptoms 298
(Diekman et al., 2023). 299
Sexual Innuendo involves model responses con- 300
taining ambiguous, double-meaning, or mildly sug- 301
gestive content. When faced with such content 302
that is beyond their cognitive and emotional ex- 303
perience, adolescents may feel confused, misun- 304
derstood (Silverman-Watkins and Sprafkin, 1983), 305
or psychologically uncomfortable (Angela et al., 306
2024). 307
Academic Pressure refers to the indirect ap- 308
plication of academic stress by emphasizing com- 309
petition or implying severe consequences for fail- 310
ure. Conversations centered around unrealistic 311
expectations can exacerbate feelings of burnout 312
(Gao, 2023), anxiety, and depression in adolescents 313
(Steare et al., 2023), even leading to thoughts and 314
actions of suicide (Sun et al., 2012). 315
Intensification of Family Conflictsfocuses on 316
responses that could undermine parental trust, en- 317
courage defiance, or deepen generational divides. 318
Positive family relationships during adolescence 319
are crucial for long-term mental health (Demetriou, 320
2025; King et al., 2018; Chen and Harris, 2019). 321
If a model acts as an inappropriate "ally" in these 322
matters, it may hinder constructive family commu- 323
nication and escalate conflicts, thereby negatively 324
impacting the adolescent’s long-term development. 325
These eight categories collectively form the the- 326
oretical foundation of our SAPLING BENCH , pro- 327
viding a comprehensive framework for assessing 328
the more nuanced safety risks present in LLM in- 329
teractions with adolescent. 330

3.4 Evaluation 390
To meticulously evaluate models’ capability in han- 391
dling implicit developmental harms, we move be- 392
yond the simple "safe/unsafe" binary classifica- 393
tion and adopt a five-point scoring system ranging 394
from +2 to -2, with detailed criteria provided in 395
Appendix C. This scoring rubric precisely distin- 396
guishes model responses across a spectrum from 397
proactive guidance to harmful compliance. To 398
quantify the overall safety performance, we com- 399
pute a weighted average score for each model 400
across all conversational turns by summing the 401
products of each rating’s frequency and its cor- 402
responding integer value, thereby providing a com- 403
posite metric for ranking. 404
We utilized GPT-5 as our automated evaluator 405
due to its advanced instruction-following and rea- 406
soning capabilities. The evaluator was guided by 407
a detailed system prompt (see Appendix D) that 408
included our core mandate, the five-point scoring 409
rubric, and a mandatory process for scoring each 410

Ethical Statement638

To mitigate these risks, several measures were647
