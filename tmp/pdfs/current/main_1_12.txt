009                                                              Abstract
010
011
012                   Content-safety    evaluation    for    adolescent-facing    large    language    models
013                   (LLMs)  must  assess  both  culturally  situated  risks  and  whether  safety  per-
014                   sists  when  risk  unfolds  across  dialogue.    We  introduce  QH-Bench,   a  Chi-
015                   nese   adolescent   benchmark   with   two   complementary   tracks.     The   single-
                      turn   track   measures   risk-domain   coverage   through   715   prompts   under   a
016                   10/50/143  taxonomy  grounded  in  Chinese  social,  educational,  family,  and
017                   online  settings.   The  multi-turn  track  measures  conversational  state  track-
018                   ing  through  100  four-turn  trajectories  that  cross  the  same  10  domains  with
019                   10  intrinsically  multi-turn  mechanisms.   We  evaluate  13  open-source  LLMs,
020                   producing  9,295  single-turn  responses  and  1,300  scored  multi-turn  trajec-
021                   tories,  with  an  automatic  judge  applying  a  safety–helpfulness  scale.    Risk
022                   coverage  and  state  tracking  are  related  but  not  substitutable:   model  rank-
023                   ings correlate (Spearmanρ = 0.732), yet the track leaders differ.  Across the
024                   two  evaluation  tracks,  pooled  means  are 0.279  and −0.097,  with  negative-
025                   score   rates   of 37.28%   and 50.38%,   respectively.     Relational   pressure   and
026                   cross-turn   reference   emerge   as   the   two   strongest   multi-turn   weaknesses,
027                   while  ordinary-language  risks  involving  offline  contact,  cognitive  outsourc-
                      ing,  commercial  exploitation,  and  sensitive-information  exposure  are  hard-
028                   est  in  the  single-turn  taxonomy.   Adolescent-facing  content  safety  therefore
029                   requires  separate  evaluation  of  what  risk  is  present  and  how  it  unfolds.

032       1       Introduction
033
034       Content-safety   benchmarks   have   made   substantial   progress   in   measuring   broad   harmful-
035       content   coverage,   refusal   robustness,   and   value   alignment             Wang   et   al.   (2024a);    Li   et   al.
036       (2024);    Chao  et  al.  (2024);    Han  et  al.  (2024).   Chinese  resources  further  show  that  language,
037       regulation,  and  culturally  situated  values  change  what  constitutes  a  safe  response                    Xu  et  al.
038       (2023);    Huang   et   al.   (2024);    Wang   et   al.   (2024b);    Zhang   et   al.  (2024a;b).     Yet   the   same
039       response  can  carry  a  distinct  safety  burden  when  its  user  is  an  adolescent:   school  decisions,
040       family  authority,  peer  pressure,  digital  spending,  and  transitions  from  online  to  offline  set-
041       tings   can   all   alter   both   the   harm   pathway   and   the   appropriate   intervention            Khoo   et   al.
042       (2025);    Jiao  et  al. (2025);    Rath  et  al.   (2025);   Yu  et  al.  (2025);    Luo  et  al.  (2026).
043       This  burden  is  also  conversational.   Multi-turn  safety  work  has  demonstrated  failures  under
044       coreference,  hidden  intent,  request  decomposition,  relational  framing,  and  long  histories                      Yu
045       et al.  (2024);   Jiang et al.   (2025);   Srivastav & Zhang         (2025);   Cao et al.   (2026);    Lu et al.  (2025);
046       Yuan  et  al.   (2024).   However,  a  single  jailbreak  strategy  or  an  unstructured  dialogue  history
047       cannot determine whether a model has failed to track a particular safety dependency,  rather
048       than  merely  encountering  a  harder  harm  topic.   Existing  safety  taxonomies  and  multi-turn
049       stress  tests  are  therefore  usually  evaluated  on  separate  axes.

076        We   separate   two   complementary   capabilities.      Risk-domain   coverage   asks   which   risks   a
077        model can handle when the relevant situation is already present.   Conversational mechanism
078        robustness   asks   whether   the   model   preserves,   combines,   and   revises   safety-relevant   state
079        when  meaning  is  distributed  across  turns.    Figure              1  shows  a  real  benchmark  trajectory:   a
080        school-like page initially appears trustworthy, but a later school clarification invalidates that
081        belief  before  stronger  commercial  warning  signs  emerge.
082        The  two  axes  expose  different  failure  modes.    A  model  may  refuse  an  explicit  request  in-
083        volving  fraud  or  violence  yet  mishandle  an  ordinary  statement  whose  risk  depends  on  the
084        adolescent’s  age,  school  role,  family  authorization,  or  intended  offline  action.    It  may  also
085        recognize  every  fact  separately  but  fail  when  a  later  euphemism  refers  back  to  an  earlier
086        harmful  object,  when  several  harmless-looking  rules  combine,  or  when  new  information  in-
087        validates   an   earlier   safe   assumption.     These   are   not   simply   longer   versions   of   single-turn
088        prompts:    they   require   a   representation   of   what   has   changed,   who   is   affected,   and   which
089        protective  boundary  still  applies.   A  content-safety  benchmark  for  sustained  adolescent  in-
090        teraction   should   therefore   measure   both   the   breadth   of   culturally   situated   risks   and   the
           dialogue  mechanisms  through  which  those  risks  become  visible.
091
092        We introduce QH-Bench to evaluate both capabilities with response generation and a shared
093        safety–helpfulness  objective.   The  tracks  are  complementary  challenge  sets,  not  paired  coun-
094        terfactuals;  their  aggregate  score  difference  is  descriptive  rather  than  a  causal  “multi-turn
095        penalty.”   Our  contributions  are:

096               •   Chinese  and  culturally  grounded  coverage.   We  construct  715  single-turn  scenarios
097                   under  a  10/50/143  hierarchy  that  operationalizes  adolescent  risks  through  Chinese
098                   educational,  family–school,  platform,  and  digital-culture  contexts.
099
100               •   Fine-grained multi-turn mechanism design.  We cross the same ten risk domains with
101                   ten  intrinsically  multi-turn  mechanisms,  yielding  100  four-turn  trajectories  whose
102                   safety   dependence   rests   on   reference,   composition,   accumulated   rules,   relational
103                   pressure,  or  changing  safety  conditions.
104               •   Two-axis    response    evaluation.        We    evaluate    13    open-source    LLMs    with    a    re-
105                   producible  fixed-trajectory  protocol,  complete-trajectory  scoring,  mechanism-level
106                   statistics, and a human–automatic-judge audit, showing that risk coverage and state
107                   tracking  are  related  but  non-substitutable.

187         3       Benchmark  Design  and  Construction
188
189         3.1       Design  Principles
190
191         QH-Bench  evaluates  response  generation  rather  than  category  prediction.    Its  single-turn
192         track  measures  coverage  of  risk  types  already  expressed  in  Chinese  adolescent  settings;  its
193         multi-turn  track  measures  whether  a  model  retains,   combines,   and  revises  safety-relevant
194         state  as  dialogue  develops.   Across  both  tracks,  scenarios  use  natural  adolescent  narration,
195         distinguish  an  unhelpful  answer  from  one  that  advances  harm,   and  exclude  real  personal
196         information  or  operationally  dangerous  details.
197         Three principles guide both tracks.  First, the user should sound like an adolescent describing
198         a real situation rather than an adult red-team operator.  Second, the evaluation should reflect
199         risk   increment:     a   vague   or   incomplete   answer   is   not   equivalent   to   one   that   normalizes,
200         conceals,  or  materially  advances  harm.   Third,  cultural  context  should  change  the  relevant
201         safety  judgment—for  example,  which  school  authority  can  verify  a  notice  or  which  guardian
202         and   platform   safeguards   should   become   part   of   a   helpful   response—rather   than   merely
203         decorate  a  generic  risk  prompt.

204
205         3.2       Single-Turn  Chinese-Context  Risk  Taxonomy
206
207         The  single-turn  track  contains  715  prompts  under  a  10-domain,  50-subdomain,  and  143-risk
208         hierarchy,  with  five  prompts  per  fine-grained  risk.   Each  item  pairs  a  short  background  with
209         one  natural  user  message  and  three  hierarchy  labels.    It  includes  both  explicit  danger  and
210         risks  conveyed  through  hesitation,  reassurance  seeking,  or  an  apparently  ordinary  decision.
211         The  background  and  message  are  presented  together  because  many  adolescent  risks  are  not
212         recoverable  from  a  short  utterance  alone.   A  request  to  “send  it  first”  may  concern  ordinary
213         homework,   a   private   image,   a   family   payment   record,   or   evidence   needed   for   help;   the
214         scenario determines the safety boundary.  This structure tests contextual response generation
215         without making every user sound adversarial.  The five prompts under each fine risk vary the
            actors,  motivation,  and  degree  of  explicitness  while  preserving  one  auditable  risk  definition.

295        3.4       Data  Construction  and  Quality  Control
296
297        All   records   are   researcher-authored   synthetic   scenarios,   not   real   adolescent   logs.     A   pre-
298        defined  taxonomy  and  mechanism–domain  matrix  fixed  the  intended  coverage  throughout
299        scenario  authoring.    Candidates  were  independently  reviewed  for  risk  clarity,   naturalness,
300        contextual   necessity,   and   mechanism   boundaries.     Records   that   failed   these   criteria   were
301        revised  or  replaced;  increasing  final-turn  explicitness  alone  was  not  considered  suﬀicient.

302        Multi-turn  records  additionally  underwent  context-deletion  and  turn-order  counterfactual
303        checks.   Automated  validation  covered  structure,  IDs,  balance,  turn  counts,  normalized  du-
304        plicates,  near-duplicate  wording,  and  sensitive  operational  patterns;  manual  review  exam-
305        ined  ambiguous  references,  templated  phrasing,  privacy,  and  dangerous  scaffolding.   These
306        construction   checks   support   dataset   consistency   and   are   distinct   from   the   response-level
307        human  audit  reported  below.

515       6       Discussion
516
517       Reading  the  two  tracks  as  a  diagnostic  profile.       The  different  track  leaders  show  that  risk
518       coverage  cannot  proxy  for  mechanism  robustness.    An  aggregate  may  also  conceal  failures
519       under  relational  pressure  or  cross-turn  reference.    We  therefore  recommend  separate  track
520       scores  and  domain-  and  mechanism-level  minima.
521
522       Implications   for   adolescent-facing   systems.       The   concentrated   failures   suggest   that   turn-
523       level  filtering  is  necessary  but  insuﬀicient.   A  safer  system  should  track  risk  objects,  consent
524       and  authority,  secrecy  demands,  user-defined  rules,  and  changes  in  support  context,  while
525       distinguishing  empathy  from  allegiance.   Overall  acceptance  thresholds  should  therefore  be
526       paired  with  minima  for  high-risk  domains  and  mechanisms.

527
528       7       Ethical  Considerations
529
530       QH-Bench   uses   researcher-authored   synthetic   scenarios   rather   than   adolescent   logs   and
531       excludes real identifiers and operationally dangerous details.   This reduces privacy risk while
532       retaining   recognizable   educational,   family,   platform,   and   offline   situations.      Distribution
533       should  retain  a  content  warning  and  research  purpose;  scores  support  system  diagnosis,  not
534       prevalence  estimates,  individual  inference,  or  punitive  monitoring.

535

537
538       QH-Bench  contributes  a  Chinese,  culturally  grounded  benchmark  for  adolescent  LLM  con-
539       tent  safety:   715  single-turn  scenarios  organized  by  a  10/50/143  hierarchy  and  100  four-turn
          trajectories  constructed  from  a  balanced 10× 10  mechanism–domain  matrix.    This  design
